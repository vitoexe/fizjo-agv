### Verdict
Part 1: **RESOLVED-A** (for sagittal-plane trunk flexion: it is a protective, knee-unloading adaptation, not a stiff-landing risk sign; **CONTEXT-DEPENDENT** by anatomical plane: sagittal trunk flexion unloads the knee, whereas frontal-plane lateral trunk lean increases knee abduction loading and can increase under fatigue) — confidence: **high**
Part 2: **CONTEXT-DEPENDENT** (pooled 3D evidence shows no consistent effect of fatigue on dynamic knee valgus [DKV] in single-leg landings; DKV increases depend on task [bilateral drop jump in males], fatigue protocol [multi-directional agility/stair protocols vs isolated local hip abduction], measurement method [2D FPPA vs 3D kinematics/moments], and time point [initial contact vs 60 ms/peak]) — confidence: **high**
Part 3: **UNRESOLVED** (**NO DATA FOUND** for any training RCT comparing muscular endurance vs maximal strength training on landing mechanics or injury; independent observational, RCT-mediation, and prospective cohort studies do not support the claim that hip or trunk endurance is more important than maximal strength for landing control or injury risk) — confidence: **high**

### Sagittal trunk flexion vs lateral trunk lean under fatigue
1. **Why the contradiction in Part 1 occurred:** The apparent conflict between Vermeulen et al. 2023 [S95] ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.5114/jhk/159460), [PMID 37181257](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37181257/)) and the "trunk-control risk" literature (Zazulak et al. 2007 [S65], [PMID 17468378](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17468378/); Hewett et al. 2009 [S64], [PMID 19372088](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19372088/)) is an artifact of conflating **sagittal-plane forward trunk flexion** with **frontal-plane (coronal) lateral trunk lean**, compounded by a misreading of Hewett et al. 2009 [S64] `EVIDENCE:review` `EVIDENCE:observational`.
2. **Even in ACL injury video analyses, an upright trunk (lack of sagittal flexion) is the injury pattern:** In the full text of Hewett et al. 2009 [S64] ([PMC4062295](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4062295/)), female athletes who ruptured their ACL actually landed with **significantly less forward trunk flexion** at initial contact ($1.6 \pm 9.3^\circ$, i.e., upright) than uninjured female controls ($14.0 \pm 7.3^\circ$, $p = 0.005$) `EVIDENCE:observational` `ANALOGY:team sports`. A second video analysis from the NIH/Boden group (Sheehan et al. 2012, $n = 40$, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0363546512437850), [PMID 22383659](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22383659/)) confirmed that ACL-injured athletes landed with a smaller forward trunk flexion angle ($4 \pm 14^\circ$ vs $16 \pm 13^\circ$, $p = 0.016$) and their center of mass positioned $38\text{ cm}$ more posteriorly relative to the base of support ($p < 0.001$) `EVIDENCE:observational` `ANALOGY:team sports`.
3. **Does sagittal trunk flexion reduce or increase knee loading?** Across three independent laboratory groups in non-fatigued landings—Blackburn & Padua 2008/2009 [S43/S44] ($n = 40$, [PMID 18037546](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18037546/), [PMID 19295962](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19295962/)), Scattone Silva et al. 2017 [S45] ($n = 21$, [PMID 28704344](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28704344/)), and Shimokochi et al. 2013 ($n = 20$, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00167-012-2011-9), [PMID 22543471](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22543471/))—voluntary forward trunk flexion consistently **reduces peak vGRF** ($3.9 \pm 0.7$ vs $5.3 \pm 0.8\text{ BW}$ in Shimokochi 2013, $p < 0.01$), **reduces quadriceps sEMG**, **reduces internal knee extensor moments** (upright landing was $56\%$ higher than forward-leaning landing, $p < 0.01$), **reduces patellar tendon force**, and **increases concomitant hip and knee flexion** `EVIDENCE:observational`. Under fatigue, two independent studies directly confirm that this sagittal trunk flexion is protective:
   - Whyte et al. 2018 ($n = 28$, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000002567), [PMID 29543702](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29543702/)) found that high-intensity intermittent fatigue **increased sagittal trunk flexion** throughout stance ($p < 0.001$) and **reduced internal knee extensor moments** ($p = 0.006$), whereas unanticipated cutting prevented this trunk flexion ($p < 0.001$) and **increased** internal knee extensor moments ($p = 0.002\text{–}0.007$) `EVIDENCE:observational`.
   - Wong et al. 2020 ($n = 12$ female athletes, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2478/hukin-2019-0122), [PMID 32269643](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32269643/)) showed that when fatigued athletes **failed** to flex their trunk forward (trunk flexion at IC decreased from $90.62 \pm 3.63^\circ$ to $86.04 \pm 4.42^\circ$, $p = 0.001$), knee flexion ROM dropped $-9.8\%$ ($p = 0.010$), **peak vGRF increased** ($2.98 \to 3.11\text{ BW}$, $p = 0.006$), and **anterior tibial shear force increased** ($0.48 \to 0.53\text{ BW}$, $p = 0.021$) `EVIDENCE:observational`.
   - Pooled 3D meta-analyses also refute the Gemini Deep Research claim that fatigue causes "less hip and knee flexion = stiff landing": Benjaminse et al. 2019 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-019-01052-6), [PMID 30659497](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30659497/)) found that while knee flexion at IC decreased in hops/cutting, **peak knee flexion** ($ES = -1.27$, $p = 0.0005$) and **peak hip flexion** ($ES = -0.48$, $p = 0.0023$) **increased** during single-leg drop landings after fatigue, and Liu et al. 2025 ($n = 496$, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0321925), [PMID 40334188](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40334188/)) found that fatigue **decreased peak knee extension moment** ($SMD = 0.64$, $p < 0.001$) `EVIDENCE:review`.
4. **Does fatigue increase LATERAL (frontal-plane) trunk lean?** Yes, in specific single-leg and cutting tasks, though Vermeulen et al. 2023 [S95] ([PMC10170549](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10170549/)) graded the overall evidence as **inconsistent (level 3)** because only 3 of 7 frontal-plane studies in their review showed a significant increase `EVIDENCE:review`. Specifically, fatigue increased ipsilateral lateral trunk flexion during single-leg drop landings in Liederbach et al. 2014 ($n = 80$, $p < 0.001$, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0363546514524525), [PMID 24595401](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24595401/)) and after isolated hip-abductor fatigue in Kim et al. 2021 ($n = 10$, from $0.34 \pm 6.41^\circ$ to $3.80 \pm 6.48^\circ$, $p < 0.001$, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.knee.2021.05.007), [PMID 34134079](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34134079/)), as well as lateral trunk lean during cutting in Whyte et al. 2018 ($n = 28$, $p = 0.038$, [PMID 29543702](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29543702/)) `EVIDENCE:observational`.

### Fatigue and knee valgus: Carcia vs Patrek
1. **Why Carcia 2005 [S122] and Patrek 2011 [S123] reached opposite conclusions:**
   - **Task (Bilateral Drop Jump vs Single-Leg Landing):** Carcia et al. 2005 ($n = 20$, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1123/jsr.14.4.321)) tested a **bilateral (double-leg) drop jump** from 30 cm, where the pelvis is supported by both legs and lateral trunk sway over a single stance hip is constrained. Patrek et al. 2011 ($n = 20$ women, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.4085/1062-6050-46.1.31), [PMID 21214348](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21214348/), [PMC3017486](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3017486/)) tested a **single-leg drop landing** from 40 cm. In a single-leg landing with fatigued hip abductors, athletes compensate by shifting their trunk laterally (ipsilaterally) over the stance hip (Trendelenburg-type compensation). As subsequently proven in full 3D/OpenSim modeling by Kim et al. 2021 ($n = 10$, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.knee.2021.05.007), [PMID 34134079](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34134079/)), this ipsilateral trunk lean ($+3.46^\circ$, $p < 0.001$) shifts the ground reaction force vector medially toward the knee joint center, **reducing the external knee abduction moment** ($0.94 \to 0.79\text{ Nm/kg}$, $p = 0.002$) or external knee adduction moment ($-27\%$ in Patrek 2011, $p < 0.05$) without changing peak 3D knee abduction angle ($p = 0.923$) or simulated ACL force ($p = 0.414$) `EVIDENCE:observational`.
   - **Time point & magnitude of change:** Carcia et al. 2005 found a statistically significant valgus increase **only at initial contact (IC)**, and its magnitude was **$<1^\circ$** (right leg $-0.19^\circ \to -0.93^\circ$; left leg $-0.42^\circ \to -1.24^\circ$, $p < 0.05$), whereas **peak valgus angle** (right $-10.28^\circ \to -9.63^\circ$; left $-12.79^\circ \to -12.86^\circ$), frontal-plane excursion, and vGRF **did not change** (data extracted in He et al. 2025 full text, [PMC12188353](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12188353/)) `EVIDENCE:review` `EVIDENCE:observational`. Patrek et al. 2011 [S123] measured at **IC** (no change: $-1.6 \pm 2.5^\circ$ vs $-1.6 \pm 2.6^\circ$) and **60 ms post-IC** (a $-0.4^\circ$ reduction in abduction angle, $0.6 \pm 3.5^\circ \to 0.2 \pm 3.6^\circ$, $p < 0.05$) `EVIDENCE:observational`. Similarly, Geiser et al. 2010 ($n = 20$ women, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0b013e3181b7b227), [PMID 19952814](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19952814/)) found only a $1.4^\circ$ increase in abduction ROM after isolated hip-abductor fatigue `EVIDENCE:observational`. Thus, across all three 3D studies (Carcia 2005, Geiser 2010, Patrek 2011), isolated hip-abductor fatigue altered 3D frontal-plane knee angles by **$\le 1.4^\circ$**.
   - **2D FPPA vs 3D angles (why Lagouvardou 2026 [S120] differs):** Lagouvardou et al. 2026 ($n = 23$ women, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbmt.2025.10.023), [PMID 41927185](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41927185/)) measured **2D Frontal Plane Projection Angle (FPPA)** rather than 3D joint angles `EVIDENCE:observational`. Because He et al. 2025 ([PMC12188353](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12188353/)) and Kim et al. 2021 ([PMC8503925](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8503925/)) show that peripheral fatigue increases **hip internal rotation, knee internal rotation, and sagittal knee flexion**, 2D camera projection registers transverse-plane rotation + sagittal flexion as apparent "2D valgus" (FPPA) even when true 3D tibiofemoral abduction does not increase `EVIDENCE:review` `BIOMECH-INFERENCE`.
2. **Which pattern the pooled evidence supports:**
   - Three independent systematic reviews with meta-analyses—**Benjaminse et al. 2019** (18 meta-analyzed 3D studies, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-019-01052-6), [PMID 30659497](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30659497/)), **Liu et al. 2025** (14 3D studies, $n = 496$, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0321925), [PMID 40334188](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40334188/)), and **Abbasi et al. 2025 [S72]** (29 2D/3D studies, $n = 683$, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13102-025-01205-3), [PMID 40605118](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40605118/))—all agree that **in single-leg landings and single-leg drop jumps, pooled fatigue effects on peak knee abduction angle and peak knee abduction moment are statistically non-significant** `EVIDENCE:review`.
   - In Abbasi et al. 2025 [S72] ([PMC12220483](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12220483/)), fatigue significantly increased peak DKV **only in bilateral vertical drop jumps** (driven by male subgroups, $p < 0.001$) and when induced by **multi-directional agility drills or stair climbing** rather than isolated single-joint fatigue `EVIDENCE:review`. (Note also that Abbasi 2025 [S72] has a lower methodological weight than Benjaminse 2019 and Liu 2025 because Abbasi pooled 2D and 3D angles together and misclassified Patrek 2011 in Table 2 and text [full text]).

### Endurance vs maximal strength
1. **Training RCTs comparing endurance vs maximal strength on landing mechanics or injury:** **NO DATA FOUND.** No randomized controlled trial was identified in PubMed, PMC, or Crossref that directly compares a muscular-endurance (high-repetition/low-load) training protocol against a maximal-strength (high-load) training protocol for the hip or trunk muscles with landing biomechanics or lower-extremity injury as the outcome.
2. **Primary peer-reviewed studies from independent groups testing both strength and endurance:** Three independent research groups have evaluated both maximal/eccentric hip strength and hip/trunk muscular endurance within the same study, and **all three found that strength—not endurance—was the significant predictor or mediator**:
   - **Jacobs et al. 2007** (Univ. of Kentucky, $n = 30$: 15 F, 15 M; [PMID 17597947](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17597947/), [PMC1896084](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1896084/)) measured both isometric hip abductor **peak torque (PT)** and **percentage of endurance capacity (%E, holding 50% PT to failure)** alongside 3D single-leg landing kinematics. Women had lower PT ($p = 0.009$) and greater peak knee valgus displacement ($7.26 \pm 6.61^\circ$ vs $3.29 \pm 3.54^\circ$, $p = 0.04$), whereas **%E did not differ between sexes**, and **hip abductor peak torque (strength) was the primary correlate of 3D knee valgus displacement in women** `EVIDENCE:observational`.
   - **Baldon et al. 2015** (Federal Univ. of São Carlos / Univ. of Pittsburgh, $n = 31$ women with PFP; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0363546515574690), [PMID 25790834](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25790834/)) conducted an 8-week RCT comparing functional stabilization training (hip/trunk strengthening + motor control) vs standard training and ran a formal mediation analysis on 3D frontal-plane single-leg squat kinematics. **Increases in eccentric gluteal torque (strength) mediated $18\%\text{ to }32\%$ of the improvements in frontal-plane trunk, pelvic, and hip kinematics**, whereas **changes in trunk muscle endurance did not significantly mediate kinematic improvements** `EVIDENCE:RCT`.
   - **Leetun et al. 2004** (Univ. of Delaware / Kentucky Sports Medicine, $n = 140$ collegiate basketball and track athletes: 80 F, 60 M; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1249/01.mss.0000128145.75199.c3), [PMID 15179160](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15179160/)) prospectively tested both baseline isometric **hip abduction and external rotation strength** and **trunk muscular endurance** (back extensor and side-bridge quadratus lumborum endurance). In logistic regression, **maximal hip external rotation strength was the only significant predictor of seasonal lower-extremity injury** ($OR = 0.86$, $95\%\text{ CI }0.77\text{–}0.97$), while **trunk endurance measures did not predict injury** `EVIDENCE:observational` `ANALOGY:basketball and track`. Likewise, Khayambashi et al. 2016 ($n = 501$, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0363546515616237), [PMID 26646514](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26646514/)) prospectively confirmed that baseline **maximal isometric hip abduction and external rotation strength** predicts noncontact ACL injury `EVIDENCE:observational` `ANALOGY:competitive team sports`.
3. **Why higher maximal strength also protects against submaximal fatigue:** As Patrek et al. 2011 [S123] ([PMC3017486](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3017486/)) demonstrated in their full text, a single-leg drop landing from 40 cm required only **$12\%$ MVIC** of the gluteus medius `EVIDENCE:observational`. Raising maximal strength (MVIC) lowers the relative percentage of maximum capacity required per landing, reducing neuromuscular fatigue accumulation per repetition `BIOMECH-INFERENCE`. Therefore, the Lagouvardou 2023/2026 [S119/S120] claim ("endurance > maximal strength") remains uncorroborated by any independent group.

### Fatigue and injury timing (prospective data)
1. **ACL ruptures do NOT increase late in matches or late in seasons:** A meta-analysis of 15 epidemiological studies (21 datasets) by Doyle et al. 2021 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1097/JSM.0000000000000752), [PMID 31415020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31415020/)) and the synthesis by Bourne et al. 2019 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-019-01134-5), [PMID 31183767](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31183767/)) found **no significant difference in ACL, groin, or hamstring injury likelihood between the first and second halves of matches or across game quarters** `EVIDENCE:review` `ANALOGY:team sports`. Furthermore, in a video analysis of $n = 134$ consecutive ACL ruptures in professional male soccer (Della Villa et al. 2020, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2019-101247), [PMID 32561515](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32561515/)), **$62\%$ of ACL tears occurred in the first half** ($p < 0.01$), when athletes move at higher speeds and generate larger ground reaction forces `EVIDENCE:observational` `ANALOGY:professional soccer`.
2. **By contrast, acute ankle sprains and overall match injuries DO increase late in matches:**
   - In the prospective UEFA study of 23 clubs over 7 seasons ($4483$ injuries in $566,000\text{ h}$; Ekstrand et al. 2011, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsm.2009.060582), [PMID 19553225](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19553225/)), **overall match injury incidence rose significantly with time elapsed in both the first and second halves** ($p < 0.0001$) `EVIDENCE:observational` `ANALOGY:professional soccer`.
   - Crucially for AGV (where the ankle is the primary injured joint in Hilgert et al. 2004 [S01], [PMID 15592983](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15592983/)), a systematic review and meta-analysis of 8 surveillance studies ($n = 500$ ankle sprains across 5 sports; de Noronha et al. 2019, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1123/jsr.2017-0279), [PMID 29809104](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29809104/)) demonstrated that **ankle sprains occur significantly more frequently in the second half ($56\%$, $95\%\text{ CI }0.50\text{–}0.62$) than in the first half ($44\%$, $0.38\text{–}0.50$)**, and double after the first quarter ($14\%$ in Q1 vs $28\%$ in Q2, $25\%$ in Q3, and $29\%$ in Q4) `EVIDENCE:review` `ANALOGY:field and court sports`.

### Practical implications for AGV (each labelled; BIOMECH-INFERENCE / ANALOGY / SPECULATION where no AGV data)
- **(a) Teach sagittal trunk flexion on landing—do NOT warn against forward trunk lean when fatigued, but cue against lateral trunk tilt and upright "stiff" landings:** Forward trunk flexion accompanied by hip and knee flexion is a primary mechanical strategy to reduce peak vGRF, quadriceps dominance, patellar tendon force, and internal knee extensor moments in both rested ([S43](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18037546/), [S44](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19295962/), [S45](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28704344/), [Shimokochi 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22543471/)) and fatigued ([S95](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37181257/), [Whyte 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29543702/)) landings `EVIDENCE:review` `EVIDENCE:observational`. Conversely, landing with an upright trunk under fatigue increases peak vGRF and anterior tibial shear force ([Wong 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32269643/)), and lateral (frontal-plane) trunk lean over a single leg increases knee joint moments ([S64](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19372088/), [Liederbach 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24595401/)) `EVIDENCE:observational`. Because hard AGV boots restrict ankle dorsiflexion (and reduced ankle dorsiflexion under mental or physical fatigue increases vGRF; [Zheng 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40497248/)), skaters must rely even more heavily on proximal sagittal hip and trunk flexion to absorb impact energy when landing from ledges, rails, and gaps `BIOMECH-INFERENCE` `SPECULATION` *(no direct study on AGV boot dorsiflexion restriction or boot mass during drop landings exists)*.
- **(b) Recommend stopping high-consequence tricks ("one last try") late in a fatigued session primarily to protect the ANKLE and prevent bails/falls—not because of a proven late-session spike in ACL valgus ruptures:** Prospective match data show that ACL ruptures do not spike late in games ([Doyle 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31415020/), [Della Villa 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32561515/)), whereas **acute ankle sprains** ([de Noronha 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29809104/)) and **overall traumatic injuries** ([Ekstrand 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19553225/)) increase significantly in the latter stages of play `EVIDENCE:review` `EVIDENCE:observational` `ANALOGY:field and court sports`. Given that ankle/foot injuries are a primary injury site in AGV ([S01](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15592983/)) and failed skateboard rail landings produce $12.09 \pm 2.63\text{ BW}$ vs $7.98 \pm 1.32\text{ BW}$ in successful landings ([S52](https://doi.org/10.1080/19424280.2010.525534)), stopping high-risk grind or gap attempts when fatigued is strongly justified to avoid missed lock-ons, bails, and acute ankle trauma `BIOMECH-INFERENCE` `ANALOGY:skateboarding`. Practical fatigue cutoffs supported by laboratory fatigue protocols include: (1) subjective exertion reaching **Borg RPE $\ge 18\text{–}19/20$** (or session RPE $\ge 8/10$; [S123](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21214348/), [Whyte 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29543702/)), (2) a noticeable drop in pop/jump height ($\ge 10\%$ decrement used as the fatigue threshold in [Liederbach 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24595401/)), or (3) **loss of sagittal flexion cushioning** (landing noticeably stiffer/more upright with louder impact; [Wong 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32269643/)) `EVIDENCE:observational` `BIOMECH-INFERENCE`.
- **(c) Prescribe heavy/eccentric hip and lower-limb strength training + movement retraining as the foundation, using endurance work only as a supplement—not a replacement:** Because independent RCT mediation data ([Baldon 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25790834/)) and prospective injury cohorts ([Leetun 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15179160/), [Khayambashi 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26646514/)) support maximal/eccentric hip strength over trunk endurance, and because isolated hip strengthening without motor control retraining does not reliably alter 3D landing kinematics ([S110](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21765220/), [S111](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25935843/)), AGV off-skate preparation should prioritize **progressive maximal/eccentric hip and lower-extremity strengthening combined with landing/balance movement retraining** ([S114](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37304892/), [S115](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39846421/)) `EVIDENCE:review` `EVIDENCE:RCT`. Higher maximal strength directly increases the reserve above the submaximal demand of each landing ($12\%$ MVIC in [S123](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21214348/)) `BIOMECH-INFERENCE`, while local endurance work for the trunk/hip can be retained as secondary conditioning for multi-hour sessions `SPECULATION`.

### Remaining unknowns
- **No direct RCT comparing hip/trunk muscular endurance training vs maximal strength training:** Resolving Part 3 definitively would require a parallel-group RCT randomizing athletes to high-load/low-repetition hip/trunk strength training vs low-load/high-repetition endurance training (both paired with landing movement retraining) and measuring 3D landing kinetics/kinematics both rested and post-fatigue.
- **No biomechanical study on how rigid AGV boots/cuffs alter proximal trunk-hip-knee landing compensation under fatigue:** While Major et al. 2007 [S02] ([PMID 17786694](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17786694/)) measured rail landing and balance in $n = 10$ AGV skaters, no study has quantified how the mass of an AGV skate or cuff-restricted ankle dorsiflexion modifies vGRF, sagittal trunk flexion, or knee moments during drop landings from ledges/gaps.
- **No epidemiological data on injury timing within AGV or skateboarding sessions:** Unlike team sports with standardized 90-minute matches ([de Noronha 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29809104/), [Doyle 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31415020/)), no surveillance study has recorded the session duration or repetition count at which acute ankle or knee injuries occur during self-paced skatepark or street sessions.
